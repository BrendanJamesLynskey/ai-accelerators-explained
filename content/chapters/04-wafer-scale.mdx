<Layer kind="concept">

A wafer-scale engine skips the step every other chip takes: cutting the wafer into dies. Cerebras's WSE-3 is the largest square that fits on a 300 mm wafer, <V of="part.wse-3.area_mm2" fmt="int" /> mm² of silicon with <V of="part.wse-3.transistors" fmt="int" /> transistors, <V of="part.wse-3.units" fmt="int" /> cores and <V of="part.wse-3.sram_bytes" fmt="bytes" /> of SRAM, all on one piece. Cerebras builds it from <V of="part.wse-3.dies" fmt="int" /> ordinary dies and keeps them joined: its fabric crosses the scribe lines between them, so the 2-D mesh of cores runs edge to edge.

<span id="a-yield" />

Why nobody else does this is the animation's subject. Defects land on a wafer at random, a density $D_0$ per unit area. A reticle-sized die of <V of="ill.gpu_die_mm2" fmt="int" /> mm² at the illustrative <V of="ill.d0_per_cm2" fmt="num" /> per cm² expects <V of="waferYield(wse-3,0.1,814,0.015).lam_die" fmt="num" /> defects and works only <V of="waferYield(wse-3,0.1,814,0.015).die_yield" fmt="pct" /> of the time; a whole wafer expects <V of="waferYield(wse-3,0.1,814,0.015).lam_wafer" fmt="num" />, and would essentially never be perfect. Cerebras's answer is to make the defects small and survivable: each core is about <V of="part.wse-3.core_um2" fmt="int" /> µm², so a defect kills one core out of <V of="part.wse-3.units" fmt="int" />, and the design has "redundant compute cores, redundant routing, and a fail-in-place architecture that shuts flaws down and routes around them". With even an illustrative <V of="ill.spare_frac" fmt="pct" /> of cores held back, there are <V of="waferYield(wse-3,0.1,814,0.015).spare_margin" fmt="num" /> spares for every defect expected.

</Layer>

## Weights in SRAM, split across wafers

<span id="a-sram" />

The prize is memory bandwidth. Each core has <V of="part.wse-3.core_sram" fmt="bytes" /> of SRAM next to its datapath, and together they are read at <V of="part.wse-3.sram_bw" fmt="rate" />, against an H100's <V of="part.h100.off_bw" fmt="rate" /> of HBM. The cost is capacity: <V of="part.wse-3.sram_bytes" fmt="bytes" /> per wafer. Llama 3.1 8B in 16 bits fits on one wafer; the 70B model, at <V of="wafersNeeded(wse-3,llama3-70b,bf16,1,1024).need" fmt="bytes" /> with its KV cache, needs <V of="wafersNeeded(wse-3,llama3-70b,bf16,1,1024).wafers" fmt="int" />. Cerebras's inference service does exactly that: "we split them at layer boundaries and map them to multiple CS-3 systems", and "70B models fit on as few as four systems". The model's count matches.

For training Cerebras uses the other answer, **weight streaming**: the weights stay in an external MemoryX unit and stream onto the wafer one layer at a time, each weight applied to a whole batch of activations held on the wafer. That suits training's huge batches, and not one user's decode: streaming 70B 16-bit weights once over a CS-3's <V of="part.wse-3.io_bps" fmt="bits" /> of I/O takes <V of="wafersNeeded(wse-3,llama3-70b,bf16,1,1024).t_stream" fmt="time" />, about one token a second.

The CS-4, announced with three WSE-3 Turbo wafers per system and first shipments "this quarter" on the access date, doubles the bandwidth to <V of="part.wse-3t.sram_bw" fmt="rate" /> per wafer and gives a wafer-to-wafer latency of <V of="part.wse-3t.hop_s" fmt="time" />. Cerebras gives its compute only as a sparse FP16 figure. Where the model needs a compute rate for a wafer it uses that figure and marks the result "sparse", so a compute term from it is a floor: the WSE-3's dense rate is its 900,000 cores times eight FP16 lanes each times a clock Cerebras does not state.

## What the bound says, and what it leaves out

<span id="a-gap" />

The model's bound for decode on SRAM is tiny: reading 70B 16-bit weights at <V of="part.wse-3.sram_bw" fmt="rate" /> takes <V of="decode(wse-3,llama3-70b,bf16,1,1024).t_step" fmt="time" />, a ceiling of <V of="decode(wse-3,llama3-70b,bf16,1,1024).tok_s_user" fmt="num" /> tokens per second. Cerebras published <V of="pub.cerebras-70b-tps" fmt="num" />, which is <V of="val.cerebras-70b-tps.ratio" fmt="pct" /> of it. The gap is the point: once the weights are in SRAM, bandwidth stops being the limit. What is left is most plausibly latency: every layer waits for the one before it, and its partial sums travel across a mesh of hundreds of thousands of cores. The published speed implies about <V of="overhead.cerebras-70b-tps.per_layer" fmt="time" /> per layer beyond the bandwidth bound (<V of="overhead.cerebras-8b-tps.per_layer" fmt="time" /> for the 8B model), which the model does not try to explain: the clock, the mesh dimensions and the per-layer schedule are not disclosed. The same tens-of-microseconds band shows up on Groq's chips and SambaNova's, and [chapter 10](/learn/10-choosing) turns it into a per-layer floor fitted on the other machines, which predicts the wafer's speed to within a small factor where the bound misses by hundreds.

<Layer kind="maths">

With defects scattered at random at density $D_0$, the number on an area $A$ is Poisson with mean $\lambda = D_0 A$, and the chance of none is $e^{-\lambda}$:

$$
Y_{\text{die}} = e^{-D_0 A_{\text{die}}}, \qquad
Y_{\text{wafer, perfect}} = e^{-D_0 A_{\text{wafer}}}, \qquad
\text{cores lost} \approx \lambda .
$$

A wafer with $s$ spare cores survives as long as $\lambda \ll s$; at the values above, $s / \lambda$ is <V of="waferYield(wse-3,0.1,814,0.015).spare_margin" fmt="num" />. The decode bound across $w$ wafers in a pipeline is the weights over one wafer's bandwidth (each wafer reads its share in turn), plus $w - 1$ hops.

</Layer>

<Remember>

- A wafer-scale engine keeps the whole wafer: defects are made small and survivable with spare cores.
- Poisson yield: $Y = e^{-D_0 A}$, so a large die that must be perfect rarely is.
- SRAM gives enormous bandwidth and little capacity: a 70B model needs several wafers.
- With weights in SRAM, per-layer latency, not bandwidth, sets the speed.

<Keep>
  $Y_{\text{die}} = e^{-D_0 A_{\text{die}}}$: the bigger the die, the more it
  must tolerate defects.
</Keep>
