## Where each family wins and loses

**GPUs** hold the most model per chip (<V of="part.h100.off_bytes" fmt="bytes" /> of HBM per H100: the whole 70B model in FP8 on one), run anything, and decode for one user at the speed of HBM: <V of="choose(llama3-70b,1,1024,2048).0.tok_s_user" fmt="num" /> tokens per second at the bound, using <V of="choose(llama3-70b,1,1024,2048).0.util" fmt="pct" /> of the chip's arithmetic. They win by batching: the same step serves hundreds of users ([chapter 2](/learn/02-gpus-the-baseline)). Their energy per token at batch 1, <V of="choose(llama3-70b,1,1024,2048).0.j_per_token" fmt="num" /> J at the TDP, is mostly an idle chip waiting on memory.

**Systolic designs** make the same trade with a compiler instead of warps: one TPU7x reads its HBM at <V of="part.tpu-7x.off_bw" fmt="rate" />, so its bound is <V of="choose(llama3-70b,1,1024,2048).1.tok_s_user" fmt="num" /> tokens per second, and like the GPU it needs a large batch to fill its arrays ([chapter 3](/learn/03-systolic-designs)).

**Wafer-scale** and **compiler-scheduled** chips put the weights in SRAM and buy per-user speed with silicon: <V of="choose(llama3-70b,1,1024,2048).2.chips" fmt="int" /> wafers or <V of="choose(llama3-70b,1,1024,2048).3.chips" fmt="int" /> GroqChips to hold a model one H100 holds. Their published speeds, <V of="pub.cerebras-70b-tps" fmt="int" /> and over <V of="pub.groq-70b-tps" fmt="int" /> tokens per second for one user, are the fastest in this site's sources. The LPU's static schedule also makes every token take the same time.

**Reconfigurable dataflow** keeps HBM but fuses a layer into one pipeline and adds a DDR tier for many resident models: <V of="choose(llama3-70b,1,1024,2048).4.chips" fmt="int" /> SN40L sockets for the 70B model in BF16, and the closest measured speed to its bound of any family here.

**Meshes of small cores** trade the fastest memory for an open, uniform design and Ethernet built into the chip: <V of="choose(llama3-70b,1,1024,2048).5.chips" fmt="int" /> Blackhole cards, each bounded by GDDR6 at <V of="part.blackhole.off_bw" fmt="rate" />.

**Edge NPUs and FPGAs** run on a few watts and the device's own memory: the 70B model needs <V of="choose(llama3-70b,1,1024,2048).6.chips" fmt="int" /> M4-class devices even at 8 bits, while the 8B model runs on one at <V of="choose(llama3-8b,1,1024,2048).6.tok_s_user" fmt="num" /> tokens per second at the bound. FPGAs win on fixed, predictable latency and exactly-sized numbers, not on large models.

**In-memory compute** removes weight movement entirely and pays in capacity and precision: <V of="choose(llama3-70b,1,1024,2048).7.chips" fmt="int" /> research chips for the 70B model. It is a research direction, not a product family, in the sources used.

## What the families are converging on

The designs started far apart and are borrowing from each other.

- **Fast memory beside capacious memory.** The SN40L and SN50 keep three tiers (SRAM, HBM, DDR); NVIDIA's announced Groq 3 LPX racks put SRAM-only LPUs beside its GPUs, "jointly computing every layer of the AI model for every output token" ([chapter 5](/learn/05-compiler-scheduled)); Cerebras's announced CS-4 raises each wafer's SRAM bandwidth to <V of="part.wse-3t.sram_bw" fmt="rate" /> from <V of="part.wse-3.sram_bw" fmt="rate" />.
- **Narrower numbers.** Every current part here quotes an 8-bit rate or narrower: FP8 on the H100, B200, TPU7x and Trainium2, block FP8 on Blackhole, INT4 on the Hailo-10H. Each halving of the weight width halves the bytes a decode step must read ([chapter 8](/learn/08-fpgas-and-npus)).
- **The compiler owns the data movement.** XLA on the TPU, whole-layer fusion on the RDU, the static schedule on the LPU, per-core data-movement programs on Tenstorrent, scheduled DMA on AMD's AI Engines: more of what a cache and a warp scheduler decide at run time is now decided ahead of time.
- **The interconnect moves onto the chip.** NVLink, the TPU's inter-chip links, NeuronLink and Blackhole's Ethernet ports all put scale-up on the accelerator itself ([AI Racks Explained, chapter 4](https://ai-racks-explained.vercel.app/learn/04-scale-up-nvlink)).

<Layer kind="maths">

For each family the model takes the fewest chips $n = \lceil (W + K)/C \rceil$ whose memory $C$ holds the weights $W$ and the KV cache $K$, then bounds one decode step by

$$
t_{\text{token}} \ge \max\!\left(\frac{W_{\text{read}} + K}{g\,\beta}, \frac{F}{g\,P}\right) + h\, t_{\text{hop}},
$$

where $g$ chips share each step (all $n$ for a tensor layout, one stage's group for a pipeline), $\beta$ and $P$ are one chip's memory bandwidth and peak rate, and $h$ hops of latency $t_{\text{hop}}$ are added where the vendor gives the latency. Decode utilisation is $F / (t_{\text{token}}\, n\, P)$. For the crossbar chip the step is instead $L$ layers of four analogue multiplies, $4L\, t_{\text{MVM}}$.

</Layer>
