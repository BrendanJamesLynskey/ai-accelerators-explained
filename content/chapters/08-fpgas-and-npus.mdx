<Layer kind="concept">

The eighth family is defined less by an architecture than by a budget. An **edge NPU** sits inside a phone, laptop or camera chip and has a few watts and whatever memory the device has. An **FPGA**, or an adaptive SoC that pairs programmable logic with arrays of small AI engines, is used when a design needs hardware shaped to one model. Both meet the same wall the animation shows: at batch 1, one user's token costs one read of every weight, and these devices have the least memory bandwidth of any family here.

<span id="a-npu" />

Apple's M4 is the example NPU. Its 16-core Neural Engine is rated at <V of="part.m4.peak_int8" fmt="ops" /> (Apple does not state the format; the model treats it as the 8-bit rate), and it shares the chip's unified memory with the CPU and GPU: up to <V of="part.m4.off_bytes" fmt="bytes" /> at <V of="part.m4.off_bw" fmt="rate" />. Llama 3.1 8B with 8-bit weights is a <V of="edgeBound(m4,llama3-8b,8,1024).weights" fmt="bytes" /> read per token, so the bound is <V of="edgeBound(m4,llama3-8b,8,1024).tok_s" fmt="num" /> tokens per second, and the Neural Engine's arithmetic is busy for <V of="edgeBound(m4,llama3-8b,8,1024).t_comp" fmt="time" /> of each <V of="edgeBound(m4,llama3-8b,8,1024).t" fmt="time" /> step. The rated operations per second hardly matter for decode; the bytes do. That is why the animation steps the weight width down: at 4 bits the same model reads half as much and the bound doubles to <V of="edgeBound(m4,llama3-8b,4,1024).tok_s" fmt="num" /> tokens per second.

<span id="a-widths" />

Narrow formats are where the two halves of this family differ. An NPU runs the formats its hardware and toolchain support: Hailo's 10H module, for example, is rated at <V of="other.hailo10h.peak_int4" fmt="ops" /> in INT4 and <V of="other.hailo10h.peak_int8" fmt="ops" /> in INT8, at <V of="other.hailo10h.power_w" fmt="watts" /> typical. An FPGA can implement any width at all, 6, 5 or 3 bits, with arithmetic built for exactly that width, so a weight costs exactly its bits in memory and in logic. The animation's widths between 8 and 4 bits are the ones an FPGA can serve directly and most NPUs cannot.

## FPGAs: an HBM card, published LLM designs and the flow

<span id="a-v80" />

**The current part.** AMD's Alveo V80 is the HBM FPGA card of this generation: a Versal HBM device (XCV80) with <V of="other.v80.luts" fmt="int" /> look-up tables, <V of="other.v80.dsps" fmt="int" /> DSP slices and 132 Mb of block RAM plus 541 Mb of UltraRAM (about <V of="other.v80.sram_bytes" fmt="bytes" /> on chip), beside <V of="other.v80.off_bytes" fmt="bytes" /> of HBM2e at <V of="other.v80.off_bw" fmt="rate" />, in a card of up to <V of="other.v80.power_w" fmt="watts" /> ([AMD's specifications](https://www.amd.com/en/products/accelerators/alveo/v80/a-v80-p64g-pq-g.html)). Pick it in the animation. Llama 3.1 8B fits in its HBM at any width shown; at 4 bits the decode bound is <V of="edgeBound(v80,llama3-8b,4,1024).tok_s" fmt="num" /> tokens per second for one user, <V of="edgeBound(v80,llama3-8b,4,1024).j_per_token" fmt="num" /> J per token at the TDP, and at 3 bits <V of="edgeBound(v80,llama3-8b,3,1024).tok_s" fmt="num" />. Two things follow. The on-chip memory, large for an FPGA, holds about a hundredth of the 8B model's 8-bit weights, so an LLM on this card streams its weights from HBM like a GPU does, with a quarter of an H100's bandwidth. And AMD gives no TOPS figure, because there is none to give: the arithmetic is whatever the design builds from those DSPs and LUTs, so the model has no compute term for the FPGA, and the bound is the memory's. The earlier Alveo V70, an AI Engine part with DDR, is discontinued (in the table for comparison: <V of="edgeBound(v70,llama3-8b,4,1024).tok_s" fmt="num" /> tokens per second at 4 bits from <V of="other.v70.off_bw" fmt="rate" /> of DDR).

**What published FPGA LLM designs do with that.** FlightLLM ([Zeng et al., FPGA 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.03868)) maps whole LLMs onto an Alveo U280 with three FPGA-specific ideas: a configurable chain of DSP48 slices that skips the zeros of several sparsity patterns, an "always-on-chip decode" scheme with mixed-precision support aimed at the decode phase's memory bandwidth, and a "length adaptive" compilation method that cuts the cost of compiling for each sequence length. At batch 1 on LLaMA2-7B the authors report 6.0 times the energy efficiency and 1.8 times the cost efficiency of a V100S, and on a Versal VHK158 1.2 times an A100's throughput (their claims, not this site's runs). [Chen et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.15159) go further towards the FPGA's native style: a **spatial** design, one hardware unit per operator with data streamed between them, built from a library of HLS kernels, with an analytical model of when it beats a GPU; for GPT-2 decode on a U280 they report 1.9 times an A100's speed and 5.7 times its energy efficiency. Both results are for batch 1 and small to mid-size models, which is where the FPGA's fixed pipeline helps most: there is no batch to fill and no kernel launch to wait for.

**The flow, and why custom widths pay.** An FPGA design for a network is usually written in C++ for high-level synthesis (Vitis HLS) or generated from the trained network by a compiler. [hls4ml](https://arxiv.org/abs/1804.06913) builds networks into FPGAs for particle-physics triggers, where its example classifier answers in about 100 ns; [FINN](https://arxiv.org/abs/1612.07119) builds a streaming dataflow accelerator, one hardware stage per layer, with each layer's resources sized to a throughput target. Both rely on the FPGA's freedom to pick any number format per layer. That pays twice. Memory: a weight costs exactly its bits, which is the animation. Logic: a multiplier's size grows about as the square of its width, so a narrow multiply built from LUTs costs a fraction of a wide one, and several narrow multiplies can share one DSP slice. The author's synthesis of a systolic array's PEs ([Systolic Arrays Explained, the model page](https://systolic-arrays-explained.vercel.app/model#synthesis)) measures the square law in gates. The cost is the one that has kept FPGAs out of large-model serving: a hardware design flow, a long place-and-route run for every change, and clock speeds well below a fixed-function chip's.

<Callout kind="illustrative">

Every bound reads each weight and the 1,024-token KV cache once per token at the device's full stated bandwidth; on the M4 that bandwidth is shared with the CPU and GPU and the Neural Engine's own share is not stated. The M4's 38 trillion operations per second are used as the 8-bit rate because Apple gives no format; the compute term never decides the bound here, so the choice does not change the result. No vendor here publishes LLM speeds for these devices in the sources used, so there is no validation row. Energy is TDP times the step time where a TDP is given (the V80 and V70), and not shown otherwise.

</Callout>

<Layer kind="maths">

With $N_w$ weights of $b$ bits, a KV cache of $K$ bytes per position over a context of $c$ positions, memory bandwidth $\beta$ and peak $P$:

$$
t_{\text{token}} = \max\!\left(\frac{N_w\, b / 8 + c\,K}{\beta}, \frac{2 N_w + F_{\text{attn}}}{P}\right),
\qquad
\frac{\text{compute term}}{\text{memory term}} \approx \frac{2 N_w / P}{N_w b / (8\beta)} = \frac{16\,\beta}{b\,P}.
$$

For the M4 at 8 bits that ratio is $16 \times 120\,\text{GB/s} / (8 \times 38\,\text{T/s}) \approx 0.006$: the arithmetic is idle more than 99% of the time at batch 1.

</Layer>

<Remember>

- Edge NPUs and FPGAs live on a budget: a few watts and modest memory bandwidth.
- At batch 1 every token is one read of every weight, so bytes, not TOPS, set the speed.
- Halving the weight width nearly doubles the bound.
- An FPGA can build any width exactly, in memory and in logic; the price is a hardware design flow.

<Keep>
  Batch-1 tokens per second ≈ bandwidth ÷ bytes of weights (plus KV cache).
