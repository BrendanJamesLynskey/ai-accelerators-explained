<Layer kind="concept">

A GPU runs a model as a sequence of kernels: one for the normalisation, one for the QKV projection, one for the attention scores, and so on. Each kernel reads its input from HBM and writes its result back, because the next kernel may run on any SM at any time. A **reconfigurable dataflow** chip turns the graph into a circuit instead. Its compiler lays the operations of a whole layer out across the chip, each on its own group of compute and memory units, wired together so that one operation's results stream straight into the next. HBM then sees only what has to come from outside: the weights, the KV cache, and the layer's input and output.

SambaNova's SN40L is the example the model runs. Each socket has <V of="part.sn40l.units" fmt="int" /> Pattern Compute Units and as many Pattern Memory Units, <V of="part.sn40l.sram_bytes" fmt="bytes" /> of on-chip SRAM between them, and <V of="part.sn40l.peak_bf16" fmt="flops" /> of BF16 compute. The paper describes how the compiler fuses "hundreds of complex operations with arbitrary access patterns into a single kernel call".

The animation runs one decoder layer of Llama 3.1 8B both ways on one socket. In a 2,048-token prefill, the kernel-by-kernel layer moves <V of="layerTraffic(sn40l,llama3-8b,bf16,2048,1,2048,true,false).bytes" fmt="bytes" /> to and from HBM: the activations between operations are as big as the weights, and unfused attention writes and re-reads its whole score matrix. Fused, it moves <V of="layerTraffic(sn40l,llama3-8b,bf16,2048,1,2048,true,true).bytes" fmt="bytes" />, and because the stages overlap, the layer's time is the larger of its total memory time and its total compute time, not the sum of each kernel's: <V of="layerTraffic(sn40l,llama3-8b,bf16,2048,1,2048,true,true).t" fmt="time" /> against <V of="layerTraffic(sn40l,llama3-8b,bf16,2048,1,2048,true,false).t" fmt="time" />.

Switch the work to a decode step at batch 1 and the difference almost vanishes: <V of="layerTraffic(sn40l,llama3-8b,bf16,1,1,8192,false,false).bytes" fmt="bytes" /> against <V of="layerTraffic(sn40l,llama3-8b,bf16,1,1,8192,false,true).bytes" fmt="bytes" />. One token's activations are tiny next to the weights, which every design must read. What fusion buys in decode is not bytes but overhead: no kernel launches between operations, and weight loads overlapped with compute. The SN40L paper reports that this reaches "over 85% of HBM bandwidth", where GPU implementations "rarely exceed 50%". The model cannot see launch overhead, so it does not credit either claim; the validation below compares the paper's measured speed with the bandwidth bound instead.

## Three tiers of memory

The SN40L's second idea is its memory. Besides the SRAM and <V of="part.sn40l.off_bytes" fmt="bytes" /> of HBM at <V of="part.sn40l.off_bw" fmt="rate" />, each socket has up to <V of="part.sn40l.ddr_bytes" fmt="bytes" /> of DDR at <V of="part.sn40l.ddr_bw" fmt="rate" />. HBM holds the models being served; DDR holds many more, ready to swap in. On 16 sockets, HBM holds <V of="tiers(sn40l,llama3-8b,bf16,16).hbm_models" fmt="int" /> copies of Llama 3.1 8B in 16 bits and DDR holds <V of="tiers(sn40l,llama3-8b,bf16,16).ddr_models" fmt="int" />; loading one from DDR into HBM over the sockets' DDR bandwidth takes <V of="tiers(sn40l,llama3-8b,bf16,16).switch_s" fmt="time" />. SambaNova built its "composition of experts" serving on this: many small models resident at once, switched per request. A GPU server holds its models in HBM and swaps from host memory over PCIe.

## The IPU: bulk-synchronous tiles

Graphcore's IPU took the opposite memory bet. The first-generation GC2 has <V of="part.gc2.units" fmt="int" /> tiles, each a core with <V of="part.gc2.tile_sram" fmt="bytes" /> of its own SRAM, <V of="part.gc2.sram_bytes" fmt="bytes" /> in all at <V of="part.gc2.sram_bw" fmt="rate" />, and nothing else on the device: "the IPU offers no memory other than the distributed memories local to each tile". It runs the **bulk-synchronous parallel** model: every tile computes on its own memory, then all tiles stop at a barrier, then they exchange data on a schedule the compiler fixed, then compute again. A model must fit in the tiles' SRAM, as on a Groq chain: Llama 3.1 70B in 16 bits needs <V of="placement(gc2,llama3-70b,bf16,1,1024).chips" fmt="int" /> GC2s. Graphcore's site lists no IPU products on the access date, so the part is marked retired.

<Layer kind="maths">

For operation $i$ of a layer, let $B_i$ be the weight and KV-cache bytes it must read, $A_i$ the activation bytes it reads and writes, and $F_i$ its flops. On a chip with memory bandwidth $\beta$ and peak $P$, kernels run one after another, each bound by its own larger term, so

$$
t_{\text{kernels}} = \sum_i \max\!\left(\frac{B_i + A_i}{\beta}, \frac{F_i}{P}\right) \;\ge\; \max\!\left(\frac{\sum_i B_i}{\beta}, \frac{\sum_i F_i}{P}\right) = t_{\text{fused}},
$$

with $A_i$ gone from the fused pipeline except at the layer's input and output. For a prefill of $T$ tokens with $h$ heads, the unfused attention also writes and reads its score matrix, $2 \cdot T \cdot h \cdot \bar{c}$ activations where $\bar{c}$ is the average number of positions each token attends to ($T/2$ for a causal prompt).

In the bulk-synchronous model each superstep costs its slowest tile's compute, the barrier, and the exchange:

$$
t_{\text{superstep}} = \max_{\text{tiles}} t_{\text{compute}} + t_{\text{sync}} + t_{\text{exchange}}.
$$

</Layer>
